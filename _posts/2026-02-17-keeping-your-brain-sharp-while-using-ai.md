I use AI tools constantly. Claude for writing, Copilot for coding, various LLMs for research and exploration. My productivity is genuinely higher than it was two years ago. But there's a question that nags at me: am I getting dumber?

Not in a dramatic way. I don't feel noticeably stupider. But I notice patterns that concern me. I reach for the AI before I've thought about a problem myself. I accept answers without verifying them as often as I should. I'm not sure I could do some things anymore without the AI helping.

The research suggests this isn't just paranoia.

## What the Studies Show

MIT's Media Lab ran a study called ["Your Brain on ChatGPT"](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/) that measured brain activity during AI-assisted writing. They found 47% reduction in brain connectivity (alpha and theta waves) when using ChatGPT compared to writing without it. People wrote 60% faster but showed weaker memory recall afterward.

A [2025 study on critical thinking](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4698/15/1/6) found negative correlation between frequent AI usage and critical thinking scores. Younger users (17-25) showed higher dependency. [Another study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11020077/) introduced the term "AI-induced cognitive atrophy" (AICICA) to describe the phenomenon, alongside concepts like "cognitive debt" from outsourcing thinking to AI.

The pattern across studies: AI reduces cognitive load in the moment, which feels good, but that reduced load might mean reduced learning. [Research on pilots](https://cognitiveresearchjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41235-024-00572-8) showed automation keeps procedural skills intact but cognitive skills decline significantly.

The neuroscience explanation is that neural pathways strengthen through use and weaken without it. The "use it or lose it" principle that applies to muscles applies to cognitive skills too. If AI handles the hard thinking, those neural pathways might atrophy.

## The Calculator Precedent

When calculators became widespread in schools, [there were protests](https://medium.com/the-history-of-the-future-of-education/a-brief-history-of-calculators-in-the-classroom-4b448b7426d4). Teachers worried students wouldn't learn mental math. The same debate happened with spell checkers, search engines, and now AI.

And honestly? People today are worse at mental arithmetic and spelling than previous generations. The tools did change us. Whether that's a net negative depends on what else we gained.

But there's a difference between calculators and AI. Calculators automate arithmetic, a low-level mechanical skill. AI automates higher-order thinking, the reasoning, synthesis, and judgment that arguably define what education is for. [The concern is different](https://time.com/7276807/why-students-using-ai-avoid-learning/) because what's being offloaded is different.

I'm not sure the calculator analogy is reassuring here.

## The Extended Mind Counter-Argument

Philosopher [Andy Clark argues](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-59906-9) we're becoming "smarter hybrid thinking systems" rather than dumber individuals. The AI is an extension of our minds, like how writing extended memory and calculators extended arithmetic. We're offloading to tools so we can focus on higher-level work.

There's something to this. I can tackle more complex problems than before because I don't get stuck on implementation details. I can explore more ideas because the research phase is faster. The AI handles the grunt work so I can focus on the interesting parts.

But the extended mind thesis assumes the core capabilities remain intact while tools extend them. What if the core is actually eroding? What if my judgment is getting worse because I practice it less? The hybrid system might be more capable overall while the human part becomes weaker.

I don't know the answer. Neither do the researchers, yet.

## How I'm Trying to Balance This

Given the uncertainty, I've adopted some practices. Not because I'm sure they work but because they seem reasonable given what we know.

**Struggle first, AI second.** For problems where I want to actually learn something, I try to think about it myself before asking the AI. The research on [pretesting](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10020-8) suggests attempting something before getting help improves retention, even if your attempt fails. [Stanford's AI lab](https://ai.stanford.edu/blog/teaching/) calls this "desirable difficulty", the productive struggle that builds understanding.

This means accepting that I'll be slower sometimes. If I just need the answer and don't care about learning, I use AI immediately. If I want to understand something, I force myself to struggle first.

**Verify, don't just accept.** When AI gives me an answer, I try to check it against other sources or my own reasoning. This is partly about accuracy but also about keeping the verification muscles active. The worst habit is accepting AI output uncritically because "it's usually right."

I'm inconsistent about this. Time pressure makes me skip verification. But I'm trying to build the habit.

**Do some things the hard way intentionally.** I still write some code manually even when AI could do it faster. I still do some research by reading papers instead of asking for summaries. I still write some drafts without AI help. Not because the AI versions would be worse but because I want to keep those skills.

This is like going to the gym when your job doesn't require physical strength. The modern environment doesn't demand it, so you have to create artificial demand.

**Notice when I'm becoming dependent.** The clearest warning sign is when I can't do something without the AI that I used to do alone. If I realize I can't write a for loop without Copilot suggesting it, that's a problem. If I can't outline an argument without Claude's help, that's a problem.

When I notice this, I force myself to do that thing manually for a while. Re-establish the neural pathway before it fully atrophies.

**Use AI for the right layer of work.** There's a difference between using AI for tedious tasks (formatting, boilerplate, research compilation) versus using it for core cognitive work (reasoning, judgment, creative synthesis). [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/articles/rethinking-ai-in-knowledge-work-from-assistant-to-tool-for-thought/) frames this as "tool for thought" versus "assistant", where depth matters more than speed. The tedious stuff is safe to offload, maybe even good to offload. The core stuff is where atrophy happens.

I try to stay conscious of which layer I'm at. Am I using AI to handle the boring parts so I can focus on the interesting parts? Or am I using AI to skip the interesting parts?

## The Honest Truth

I don't know if these practices are enough. The research is early, the effects are long-term, and it's hard to measure your own cognitive decline. Maybe I'm worrying about nothing. Maybe the degradation is already happening and I can't perceive it.

What I do know is that the productivity gains from AI are real and I'm not going to stop using these tools. The question is how to capture the benefits while minimizing whatever cognitive costs exist.

The gym analogy feels apt. Our environment no longer requires physical labor, so we invented artificial physical challenges (exercise) to maintain bodies that evolved for a different context. Maybe we need artificial cognitive challenges, deliberate practices that maintain mental capabilities our tool-rich environment no longer demands.

Or maybe this is all overthinking and humans adapt to their tools just fine. Calculators didn't destroy mathematics, just changed what we do with it. Maybe AI will change what cognitive work means without destroying our capacity for it.

I'm hedging my bets by trying to keep core skills sharp while using AI for everything else. Ask me in ten years if it worked.
