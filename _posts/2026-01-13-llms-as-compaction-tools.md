There's a framing of AI writing that treats it like cheating. You're supposed to do the work, struggle with the blank page, craft each sentence by hand. Using AI to write means you didn't really write it. [Research on human-AI collaboration](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03075079.2024.2323593) shows this debate is far from settled, with academics particularly concerned about authenticity and originality.

I wrote about [the authorship question](/ai/2025/11/25/the-llmphant-in-the-room.html) before. The short version: authorship lives in the ideas, not the words. But there's another angle worth exploring. What exactly is the AI doing when it helps you write?

It's compacting.

## What Compaction Actually Means

An LLM can read a large body of text, understand it (in its pattern-matching way), and help you produce a shorter, clearer version. It can take your rambling notes and turn them into structured prose. It can take a 50-page report and extract the key points.

But here's the thing: the AI doesn't know what matters. It can identify patterns, find themes, detect repetition. It can't tell you which insights are worth keeping and which are noise. That requires judgement. Your judgement.

When you use an LLM to compress information, you're not outsourcing the thinking. You're outsourcing the serialization. The AI handles turning your decisions into words. You handle deciding what's important.

## The Compiler Analogy

I think about it like using a compiler. When I write code, I don't write machine instructions directly. I write in a high-level language that expresses my intent, and the compiler turns that into something the machine can execute. Nobody accuses me of not "really" programming because I didn't write the assembly myself.

The compiler is a serialization tool. It takes my ideas (expressed as code) and converts them into a format the machine understands. The ideas are still mine. The architecture decisions, the algorithms, the tradeoffs, all mine. The compiler just handles the translation.

Using an LLM to write is similar. I have ideas, arguments, insights that exist in my head. The LLM helps serialize them into prose that other humans can read. The ideas are still mine. The LLM handles the translation.

This isn't true for everyone using AI to write. If you ask an LLM to "write a blog post about productivity," you're outsourcing both the ideas and the serialization. The AI is doing the thinking. But if you've already done the thinking and you're using the AI to express it clearly, you're just using a better compiler.

## Why Compaction Is Valuable

The [dead internet theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Internet_theory) says AI-generated content is flooding the web with empty text that looks like knowledge but isn't. [Recent surveys](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00007) show nearly 50% of internet traffic is already automated, with predictions it could reach 90% by 2026. And that's true for a lot of AI output. Content farms pumping out SEO-optimized articles that say nothing. LinkedIn posts that hit all the engagement triggers but contain zero insight.

But compaction, good compaction done with human judgement, is the opposite of that. It's taking the signal and removing the noise. It's making knowledge more accessible, not less.

Think about what happens when you read a long research paper, understand it, and explain the key findings to a colleague in five minutes. You've compacted the information. You applied judgement to decide what matters. The original 40 pages are now a few paragraphs of actual insight.

LLMs can help with this process. They can help you structure your explanation, find clearer ways to phrase things, catch redundancy. But you did the reading, the understanding, the deciding what's important. The compaction is yours. The LLM just helped with the final serialization.

## The Lossy Compression Critique

There's a valid critique here. Jim Nielsen wrote about [AI as lossy compression](https://blog.jim-nielsen.com/2024/ai-is-like-a-lossy-jpeg/), comparing it to a heavily compressed JPEG. The image looks right at first glance but zoom in and you see artifacts everywhere. With AI text, you can't see the artifacts as easily, but they're there.

This is true when the AI is generating from nothing or from vague prompts. Ask for "an article about leadership" and you'll get something that sounds like leadership advice but may not contain any actual insight. The compression is lossy because there was nothing substantial to compress.

But when you're compacting your own thinking, the dynamic is different. You're the quality control. You can see when the AI has mangled your point or added fluff that wasn't in your original idea. You're not trusting the compression blindly, you're using it as a drafting tool and editing the result.

The lossy compression problem is about trusting AI-generated content at face value. Compaction with human judgement is about using AI as a tool while you remain the source of substance.

## What Makes Compaction Yours

The work that matters in compaction:

**Reading and understanding the source material.** The AI can summarize, but you have to actually engage with the ideas to know if the summary captured what matters. You can't outsource comprehension.

**Deciding what's important.** A 50-page report might have three insights that actually matter and 47 pages of context and hedging. The AI doesn't know which three. You do, because you understand the domain and what your audience needs.

**Recognizing what's wrong.** When the AI produces something that sounds good but misses the point, you're the one who catches it. This requires having a point to miss in the first place.

**Adding what's missing.** The AI works with what you give it. If there's a connection between ideas that you haven't made explicit, the AI won't invent it. You have to provide the insight, then the AI can help you express it.

The AI handles: word choice, sentence structure, flow, organization, removing redundancy, matching tone. These are real skills and historically they were bottlenecks for people who think clearly but struggle to write. Now they're automated.

## The Dead Internet Counter-Argument

If AI-generated content is flooding the internet, doesn't more AI writing make the problem worse? Yes and no.

Content generated without human judgement, the SEO spam, the LinkedIn fluff, the fake articles, that's noise. It makes the internet worse. Using AI to produce more of that is adding to the problem.

But content that compacts genuine human insight is signal. It's making knowledge more accessible. A clear explanation of a complex topic helps people understand things faster. Good compaction is valuable regardless of what tools produced it.

The problem with AI content isn't that AI touched it. The problem is when there's no human thinking behind it. [Research suggests](https://phys.org/news/2025-10-human-ai-authorship-protocol-aims.html) authenticity comes from the human judgment involved, not the tools used. You can tell the difference between someone using AI to serialize ideas they actually have versus someone asking AI to generate both the ideas and the prose. The first has substance. The second doesn't.

## How I Use This

For this blog, I write notes and outlines first. Ideas, arguments, examples I want to include. Sometimes bullet points, sometimes rambling paragraphs. The thinking happens in this phase, often away from the keyboard, in the shower, on walks.

Then I use Claude to help draft. I provide my notes and describe what I'm trying to say. The AI produces prose. I edit heavily, often rewriting entire sections when the AI missed my point or added filler I didn't want.

The result is faster than writing from scratch but the ideas are mine. The AI compacted my notes into readable prose. I applied judgement throughout, deciding what to keep, what to cut, what to rephrase.

Writing was always my bottleneck. I can think clearly but turning that thinking into polished prose took forever. AI removed the bottleneck. It didn't remove the thinking. As [LeadDev put it](https://leaddev.com/velocity/writing-code-was-never-the-bottleneck), writing was never the hard part, thinking is.

## Persisting Your Judgement

In a world where AI can generate infinite content, the scarce resource is judgement. What matters? What's true? What's worth paying attention to? Machines can't answer these questions. Humans can.

When you use AI for compaction, you're persisting the results of your judgement. You read, understood, decided what matters, and created something that captures that decision. The AI helped with the mechanics, but the value came from you.

That's not cheating. That's using tools effectively. And in an internet increasingly full of noise, clear human judgement serialized into readable form is exactly what we need more of.
